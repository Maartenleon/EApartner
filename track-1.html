Track 1

Architecture
for Change

Architecture that gives complex change a spine — from an integrative long-term target to the first executable step. Built for an intelligent, agentic future.

The model

Five layers,
one coherent whole

Enterprise Architecture works across five interconnected layers — from business intent at the top, to the technology foundations below. Each layer informs the next. AI and autonomous agents cut across all of them.

01

Layer 1

Strategy

Business intent, principles, and architectural boundaries that govern every decision below.

The role

Architecture starts where strategy meets structure. The EA function translates business intent — goals, priorities, constraints — into architectural boundaries that make those intentions legible at every layer below.

Without this connection, architecture becomes decoration: technically correct but organisationally irrelevant. With it, every downstream decision can be traced back to a reason.

  • Strategic principles that set real boundaries
  • Business goals translated into architectural intent
  • Guardrails that outlast individual projects
  • North Star: one-pager + scope + explicit non-goals

In an agent-first world

Agentic systems do not just execute decisions — they make them. Architecture principles must now govern not only how systems are designed, but under what conditions autonomous action is sanctioned.

If your strategy does not specify where AI can act independently and where human oversight is required, your architecture has no principled answer for the systems being built on it.

02

Layer 2

Business Capabilities

What the organisation does — independent of how it does it. The most stable layer in the architecture.

The role

A capability describes what your organisation does, not how. It is the most durable artefact in EA — a capability map built today will still be accurate in five years, even if every system behind it has changed.

The capability map is the Rosetta Stone of enterprise architecture: it translates between business intent above and application reality below. It exposes where investment is concentrated, where ownership is unclear, and where the organisation is building the same thing twice.

  • Capability model (L1/L2) with ownership per domain
  • Heat maps: maturity, investment, pain, strategic alignment
  • System of record per capability
  • Change prioritisation anchored to capability gaps

In an agent-first world

Every capability is a candidate for AI augmentation — or full agent execution. The capability map becomes the blueprint for where to deploy intelligence: which capabilities benefit from continuous optimisation or pattern recognition, and which require human judgement to stay in the loop.

Without a capability map, AI deployment is ungoverned. With one, it becomes a deliberate architectural choice.

03

Layer 3

Data & Information

Ownership, systems of record, and the semantic clarity that makes integration trustworthy.

The role

Data is the currency of integration. Without clarity on who owns which data, which system is the system of record, and what quality thresholds are acceptable, integration becomes guesswork and reporting becomes politics.

Data architecture is not about databases. It is about accountability — and the confidence that when two systems exchange data, they mean the same thing by it.

  • Data ownership model per capability domain
  • Conceptual data model + system-of-record mapping
  • Data quality definitions and acceptance criteria
  • Integration & data ownership in the target state

In an agent-first world

Data architecture is AI architecture. The accessibility, quality, and governance of your data determines exactly what agents can do — and what they will get wrong.

Agents consume data at machine pace and at scale. A data model that works for human-paced decisions will not scale to continuous autonomous reasoning. Data lineage, access controls, and semantic clarity shift from good practice to foundational requirement.

04

Layer 4

Applications

The systems that enable capabilities — and the integration landscape that connects them.

The role

Applications enable capabilities. The application landscape maps which systems serve which capabilities — and in doing so, surfaces redundancy, gaps, and integration complexity that would otherwise stay invisible until it becomes expensive.

This layer is where most legacy debt accumulates. Modernisation decisions made without an application landscape view tend to create new complexity faster than they resolve the old kind.

  • Application landscape + capability-to-application mapping
  • Integration architecture and API inventory
  • Redundancy and gap analysis
  • Modernisation sequencing with dependency mapping

In an agent-first world

Applications evolve from execution systems to orchestration surfaces. APIs become interfaces for agents — not just for frontends or integrations. The integration architecture determines what agents can reach, what they can trigger, and how independently systems can coordinate.

Systems that are not API-first become bottlenecks in the agentic stack. Application architecture now has to account for autonomous callers.

05

Layer 5

Technology & Infrastructure

The foundation. Platform choices constrain everything above them — and most technology debt lives here.

The role

Technology is the foundation. Platform choices, cloud strategy, and infrastructure decisions constrain everything above them. Debt accumulated here travels upward into every layer that depends on it — and it rarely stays localised.

Technology architecture is not infrastructure management. It is about making the right platform bets at the right moment — knowing which choices are reversible and which are not.

  • Technology radar + platform decision log
  • Build vs buy principles with rationale
  • Infrastructure standards and cloud strategy
  • Technology debt mapping and remediation sequencing

In an agent-first world

New primitives enter the stack: GPU infrastructure, model hosting, vector databases, agent runtimes, and observability tooling designed for non-deterministic systems.

These are not optional extensions to existing infrastructure — they represent foundational choices about what kinds of intelligence your architecture can support. An organisation that treats AI infrastructure as an afterthought will find it constrains every layer above it.

The thread

Architecture in an agent-first world

How the layers hold — and what fundamentally changes.

The layers do not disappear

In an AI-first world, the five layers become more important, not less. Agents need clarity that humans can work around. Ambiguous ownership, undocumented integration, and ungoverned data become active liabilities when autonomous systems act on them at scale.

Strategy must specify where autonomous action is sanctioned. Capabilities define what agents are responsible for. Data architecture governs what agents can access. Applications determine what agents can trigger. Technology provides the substrate they run on.

  • Each layer becomes a governance surface for AI
  • Architecture principles need to address autonomous action
  • Data quality and lineage become AI enablers or blockers

What changes is the direction of flow

Traditional EA thinks top-down: strategy informs capability, capability guides application, application rests on technology. Governance moves at the speed of human decision-making.

In an agentic world, execution flows bottom-up at machine speed — and the architecture must be coherent enough to contain it. Agents do not wait for governance cycles. They act within whatever boundaries the architecture has — or has not — established.

The layered model is not a constraint on AI. It is the structure that makes AI safe to deploy at scale.
